The Complete Standing Desk Setup Guide

Updated August 2026 · 8 min read · Free tool: height calculator
On this page 1. Get the heights right · 2. Monitor & keyboard · 3. Sit–stand rhythm that works · 4. Accessories worth it (and not) · 5. Seven mistakes everyone makes · 6. FAQ

A standing desk only pays off if it’s set up for your body. The good news: there are just four numbers to get right, and you can measure them all in five minutes with a tape measure.

1. Get the heights right first

Everything starts with elbow height. Stand (or sit) up straight, bend your elbows at 90–100°, and relax your shoulders. The desk surface should meet your forearms — not force them up, not make you reach down.

  • Sitting: desk height ≈ 40–43% of your body height.
  • Standing: desk height ≈ 63–68% of your body height.
  • Chair seat: ≈ 25–27% of your body height, feet flat on the floor.

Our calculator gives you personalized ranges in both cm and inches. If a preset memory button on an electric desk doesn’t match your numbers, reprogram it — those presets ship for an imaginary average user.

2. Monitor & keyboard position

  • Top of screen at eye level. Looking straight ahead, your gaze should land on the top edge or slightly below. Laptops need a riser or monitor arm — the built-in screen is always too low.
  • Distance about an arm’s length (roughly 50–75 cm). Increase font size before moving the screen closer.
  • Wrists neutral while typing. If your desk is slightly too high, a thin keyboard plus negative-tilt tray beats hunching your shoulders.
  • Re-check when switching positions. Your standing eye level is different from sitting — screens on fixed arms often need a nudge each time. This is the most common reason people “hate” standing work.

3. A sit–stand rhythm that actually sticks

Standing all day is not the goal — alternating is. Studies on sit–stand workstations consistently find the benefit comes from breaking up uninterrupted sitting, not from maximal standing time.

  • Start with 15–20 minutes of standing per hour, build up over two weeks. Feet, calves and lower back need time to adapt.
  • Anchor switches to events, not timers you’ll ignore: stand for calls, sit for deep work, walk during brainstorming.
  • Mild leg fatigue is fine; numbness, varicose-type heaviness or sore lower back means too much static standing.

5. Seven mistakes everyone makes

  1. Using the factory preset heights instead of their own numbers.
  2. Screen positioned for sitting while working standing (or vice versa).
  3. Going from 0 to 4 hours of standing on day one.
  4. Leaning on the desk with one hip — the classic “standing slouch”.
  5. Wearing soft, unsupportive shoes on hard floors without a mat.
  6. Cables stretched across the lift mechanism (also a safety issue).
  7. Treating the desk as exercise. It isn’t — regular walks still matter.

6. Quick FAQ

Should I get a leaning chair? Optional. Nice for variety, unnecessary if you alternate properly.
Laminate or bamboo desktop? Both fine at typical loads; check thickness ≥ 18 mm for stability.
Single or dual motor? Dual lifts faster and holds stability at max height better — details in our buying framework.
